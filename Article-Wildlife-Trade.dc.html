Wildlife Trade in North Africa: Reading the Data

Zakher Bouragaoui20249 min read

CITES — the Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species — tracks the legal trade of wildlife across borders. Reading that data as a time series and a taxonomic breakdown turns a compliance record into a conservation early-warning system. Below is a worked example of how I approach that kind of analysis: a mix of trend lines, category breakdowns, and a single composite score.

An Enforcement-Capacity Score

Composite indices compress many indicators — border staffing, lab turnaround, prosecution rates — into one number. They're blunt, but they're what gets shown to a funder or a minister. Here's what that looks like as a single gauge.

{{ gaugeValue }} out of 100

Regional Enforcement Capacity Index

A composite of reported staffing, inspection frequency, and case-resolution data across the sample. Below 50 typically signals a monitoring gap rather than low trade volume.

A seizure record tells you what was caught. It's the gap between that and the enforcement-capacity score that tells you what was missed.

What This Means for Monitoring

None of this replaces fieldwork — but it tells you where to point it. A taxon with a high seizure share and a region with a low capacity score is where the next monitoring investment should go, not necessarily where the loudest headline is.

This piece is a template for how I read trade data before I ever get in a truck — the full regional analysis, with verified figures and country-level breakdowns, is in progress.
