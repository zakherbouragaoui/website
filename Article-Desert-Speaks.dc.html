The Desert Speaks

An acoustic journey through 1,000 kilometres of southern Tunisia, revealing 29 species and 1,210 detections in landscapes often dismissed as silent.

Zakher BouragaouiApril 202614 min read

For two days in April 2026, a thousand kilometres of Tunisian landscape unfolded from Mediterranean transition zones to the deep Sahara. This is not a story of silence. It is a story of 29 species, 1,210 detections, and a desert that refuses to be called empty.

Chapter 01

Tunis: Departure

Every expedition begins somewhere. For this acoustic journey, that place was Tunis — the capital city where Mediterranean influence still holds, where winter rains support agriculture, where the transition to desert has not yet fully begun.

We departed in the early morning, vehicles packed with AudioMoth recorders, GPS units, field notebooks, and the cautious optimism that comes with any research venture.

What we would find at Qsar Ghilen remained uncertain. But the gradient itself was guaranteed — each kilometre south bringing measurable changes in temperature, rainfall, vegetation structure, and the biological communities adapted to these constraints.

Chapter 02

Matmata: Underground Architecture

At Old Matmata, human adaptation to desert extremes takes architectural form. The Berber community here carved homes directly into hillsides — not as novelty, but as necessity.

Passive thermal regulation

Underground dwellings maintain stable temperatures year-round — a passive thermal regulation system in a place where ambient conditions can exceed 45 °C in summer. The architecture is sustainable by design, requiring no external energy inputs for climate control.

Cultural heritage meets film tourism

The circular Galaxy Tours sticker marks where Luke Skywalker's homestead stood in the 1977 Star Wars film. Film tourism infrastructure overlays cultural heritage, creating economic opportunities while raising a question: can vernacular knowledge systems survive their transformation into spectacle?

Chapter 03

The Transit Zone

Between cultural landmarks lie the ordinary kilometres — the transit zones that most narratives skip. But landscapes in motion reveal patterns invisible from static observation points.

Techine: settlement patterns

Small settlements punctuate the route south. Each represents a calculation: where can humans persist, given available water, arable land, and connection to larger networks?

Kef el Boum: Owl Cliff

Place names encode ecological knowledge. “Kef el Boum” — Owl Cliff — announces a relationship between topography and avifauna that predates roads and power lines. A raptor perched on a roadside utility pole confirmed the toponym as we passed.

The gradient

The landscape transforms. Vegetation thins. Soil gives way to sand. The horizon extends without interruption. The road to Qsar Ghilen served for decades as Rally Dakar infrastructure.

Chapter 04

Qsar Ghilen: The Acoustic Hotspot

At Qsar Ghilen, the desert speaks loudest. Not because the landscape amplifies sound, but because water concentrates life.

The oasis hotspot

The oasis functions as a biodiversity hotspot — a concept ecologists use to describe areas with disproportionately high species richness relative to surrounding terrain. Over 17 hours of acoustic monitoring we documented 1,210 detections across 29 species, an assemblage of migrants, residents and opportunistic visitors, with peak activity at dawn (05:00–07:00) and dusk (17:00–19:00).

The acoustic grid

Passive acoustic monitoring inverts traditional field ornithology. Instead of observer presence biasing what gets recorded, autonomous units document soundscapes continuously.

The sand dunes

Beyond the oasis, dune fields stretch toward the Grand Erg Oriental — vast sand seas where wind, not water, shapes the terrain. Yet even here, life persists.

Nocturnal arthropod surveys using UV light traps revealed unexpected diversity: weevils, hawk moths, mantids, and longhorn beetles. These are not incidental species — they form the trophic foundation that sustains insectivorous birds, bats, and reptiles.

The community

No research happens in isolation. At Qsar Ghilen, Am Ali — a local guide with decades of desert experience — became collaborator, translator, and interpreter of landscape patterns we might otherwise have missed.

Traditional ecological knowledge operates on different timescales than acoustic surveys. Where our recorders captured 17 hours, Am Ali carried knowledge of seasonal rhythms, historical abundances, and behavioural patterns accumulated over a lifetime of observation.

Community engagement isn't just ethical practice — it's methodological necessity. Research conducted without local knowledge risks misinterpreting patterns, overlooking context, and producing conclusions disconnected from lived reality.

The archaeology

The Roman ruins at Qsar Ghilen tell a story of continuity. For millennia, this oasis has functioned as a node where water enables persistence.

Chapter 05

The Return Journey

Douz: gateway to the Sahara

The last major settlement before the Grand Erg Oriental, where services and human infrastructure give way to sand. The road carries multiple narratives — for decades it served as a Rally Dakar route.

Chott El Fejej: seasonal wetlands

A seasonal salt lake that fills with water in wet years, creating temporary wetland habitat critical for migratory waterfowl — and, increasingly, a site where recreational vehicle traffic poses a conservation challenge.

Bouhedma National Park

Formal protection for steppe ecosystems supporting reintroduced populations of dorcas gazelle, addax, and oryx.

Four findings

High resident population

48% of detected species are confirmed breeding residents, indicating that Qsar Ghilen supports year-round populations, not just transient visitors.

Migratory stopover site

Trans-Saharan migrants — warblers, bee-eaters, redstarts — were abundant, confirming the oasis as critical for birds crossing the Sahara.

Nocturnal community

Four nocturnal species were captured — owls and nightjars — that would be entirely missed by traditional diurnal surveys.

Conservation priority

A small oasis area supports biodiversity orders of magnitude higher than the vast surrounding desert, meriting elevated protection.

What the Desert Says

The desert speaks — if we bother to listen.

Over two days and 1,000 kilometres, we moved through landscapes often dismissed as barren. Yet our recorders documented 29 species and 1,210 detections — an acoustic signature that refutes the “empty desert” narrative.

What matters more than the species count is the recognition that biodiversity persists in systems actively shaped by human pressure — agriculture, recreation, infrastructure, climate change. Oases like Qsar Ghilen exist at the intersection of ecological and social systems.

The desert speaks. What we do with what it says remains an open question.
